1. Dose Based on Age: Young's, Cowling's and Fried's Rules

12 questions

Question 1 of 66
The usual adult dose of a drug is 500 mg. Using Young's rule, calculate the dose for a child aged 4 years.
A. 125 mg
B. 104.2 mg
C. 166.7 mg
D. 13.3 mg

A. 125 mg

Given: age = 4 years; adult dose = 500 mg.
Young's rule: Dose for child = [Age / (Age + 12)] × Adult dose
= [4 / (4 + 12)] × 500 mg = (4/16) × 500 mg = 125 mg.

Young's rule uses the age in years on top and the age plus 12 underneath; do not forget to add the 12.

Question 2 of 66
The usual adult dose of a drug is 250 mg. Using Young's rule, calculate the dose for a child aged 8 years.
A. 13.3 mg
B. 100 mg
C. 166.7 mg
D. 93.75 mg

B. 100 mg

Given: age = 8 years; adult dose = 250 mg.
Young's rule: Dose = [Age / (Age + 12)] × Adult dose
= [8 / (8 + 12)] × 250 mg = (8/20) × 250 mg = 100 mg.

An 8-year-old gets 8/20, which is 40% of the adult dose.

Question 6 of 66
The usual adult dose of a drug is 300 mg. Using Cowling's rule, calculate the dose for a child aged 5 years and 4 months.
A. 128 mg
B. 75 mg
C. 62.5 mg
D. 88.2 mg

B. 75 mg

Given: present age = 5 years 4 months, so age at next birthday = 6 years; adult dose = 300 mg.
Cowling's rule: Dose = (Age at next birthday × Adult dose) / 24
= (6 × 300 mg) / 24 = 1800 / 24 = 75 mg.

Cowling's rule uses the age the child will be at the NEXT birthday (6), not the present age (5).

Question 12 of 66
The adult dose of a drug is 400 mg. A child is 6 years old. What doses are obtained by Young's rule and by Cowling's rule?
A. Young's 133.3 mg; Cowling's 116.7 mg
B. Young's 133.3 mg; Cowling's 100 mg
C. Young's 116.7 mg; Cowling's 133.3 mg
D. Young's 200 mg; Cowling's 100 mg

A. Young's 133.3 mg; Cowling's 116.7 mg

Given: age = 6 years (next birthday = 7); adult dose = 400 mg.
Young's: [6 / (6 + 12)] × 400 mg = (6/18) × 400 = 133.3 mg.
Cowling's: (7 × 400 mg) / 24 = 2800 / 24 = 116.7 mg.

Young's uses the present age over (age + 12); Cowling's uses next-birthday age over 24, so the two rules give slightly different answers.

2. Dose Based on Weight: Clark's Rule

6 questions

Question 13 of 66
The usual adult dose of a drug is 500 mg. Using Clark's rule, calculate the dose for a child weighing 30 lb.
A. 100 mg
B. 220 mg
C. 45.5 mg
D. 2500 mg

A. 100 mg

Given: weight = 30 lb; adult dose = 500 mg.
Clark's rule: Dose = [Weight (lb) × Adult dose] / 150 (average adult weight in lb)
= (30 × 500 mg) / 150 = 15000 / 150 = 100 mg.

Clark's rule compares the child's weight in pounds with an average 150-lb adult; 30 lb is one fifth of 150.

4. Dose Based on Body Surface Area (BSA)

10 questions

Question 32 of 66
The usual pediatric dose of ephedrine sulfate is 25 mg/m². Calculate the dose for a child weighing 18 kg and measuring 82 cm in height. (BSA may be found from the formula or the nomogram below.)
A. 960 mg
B. 9.25 mg
C. 16 mg
D. 10.25 mg

C. 16 mg

Given: height = 82 cm; weight = 18 kg; dose = 25 mg/m².
BSA = √[Ht (cm) × Wt (kg) / 3600] = √(82 × 18 / 3600) = √(1476 / 3600) = √0.41 = 0.64 m²
Dose per m² given: Dose = BSA × Dose per m² = 0.64 m² × 25 mg/m² = 16 mg.
(Note: the slide writes "25/m²" without a unit; it is taken as 25 mg/m².) (Not solved in the lecture.)

When the dose is given per m², find the BSA and multiply — do not divide by 1.73 in this case.

Question 33 of 66
The pediatric dose of a drug is 30 mg/m². Calculate the dose for a child 100 cm tall weighing 25 kg.
A. 20.8 mg
B. 14.5 mg
C. 1500 mg
D. 25 mg

D. 25 mg

Given: height = 100 cm; weight = 25 kg; dose = 30 mg/m².
BSA = √(100 × 25 / 3600) = √(2500 / 3600) = √0.694 = 0.833 m²
Dose = 0.833 m² × 30 mg/m² = 25 mg.

Remember the square root: 2500/3600 is 0.694, and its square root is 0.833 m².
